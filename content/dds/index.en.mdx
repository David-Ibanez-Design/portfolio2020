<ArticleOverview>
    <Sunnary>
        Digima is a B2B SaaS CRM used by small and medium-sized Japanese real estate companies. The original version of the product had been built by a single developer who handled both design and code, with no process or design system in place. As a result, by the time I joined Comvex the product was deeply inconsistent and getting harder to extend with every new feature. The team made the decision to rebuild the product from the ground up, and I owned the design side of solving this.
    </Sunnary>
    <Problems>
        <ul>
            <li>The company needed a more scalable way to deliver feature designs.</li>
            <li>Digima users needed a more consistent user experience.</li>
            <li>Design and engineering had no shared vocabulary, which made every feature handoff slower than it should have been.</li>
        </ul>        
    </Problems>
    <Goals>
        <ul>
            <li>Create tools and processes that bring quality, consistency, and speed to the design process.</li>
            <li>Redesign the product using those tools.</li>
            <li>Reduce the distance between design intent and shipped code.</li>
        </ul>      
    </Goals>
    <Outcomes>
        <ul>
            <li>A complete design system, established from scratch and still in active use today.</li>
            <li>Significant reduction in design-to-engineering delivery time, with much higher consistency.</li>
            <li>Design tokens shipped to the frontend codebase and enforced by lint rules I authored, which block hard-coded values at merge time.</li>
            <li>A Storybook documentation layer integrated directly into the application codebase, used by around 10 engineers.</li>
            <li>A foundation in place for the next architectural step: closing the remaining design-code gap and moving toward an agentic design system.</li>
        </ul>        
    </Outcomes>
    <Role>
        <ul>
            <li>Established the design system from scratch and have led its evolution since.</li>
            <li>Drove every major architectural shift: the Sketch-to-Figma migration, the move into the codebase via tokens and lint rules, and the current shift toward AI-assisted generation.</li>
            <li>Own the system's structure, define its rules, and approve changes to it.</li>
            <li>Review and QA component contributions and refactors made by engineers.</li>
        </ul>   
    </Role>  
</ArticleOverview>

#### Audit
## The product audit revealed many inconsistencies and user experiences issues, confirming the need for a design system.

<p>
 Before proposing anything, I evaluated what already existed. I went through the full product interface and captured every menu, button, modal, and form. I organized them into a spreadsheet, grouped them into families, and gave each variant a name. What came back made the case for everything that followed:
</p>

<ul>
    <li>130 different colors in use across the product</li>
    <li>56 different shades of grey</li>
    <li>22 different font sizes</li>
    <li>4 completely different variants of the search field</li>
</ul>

<p>
Seeing the inventory laid out side by side confirmed that what we needed was a well-organized, collaborative design system built specifically for Digima's needs.
</p>

#### Principles and Visuals
## Before designing components, the team and I agreed on four principles that would let us make consistent decisions later without re-litigating each one.

<p>
Four principles emerged from team discussions and have held up ever since:
</p>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Be purposeful:</strong> Design with intention; validate with data where possible.</li>
</ul>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Provide clarity:</strong> Apply the same solution to the same problem. Change familiar patterns only when the change is a definite improvement. (Consistency trumps originality, and coherence trumps consistency.)</li>
</ul>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Design Iteratively:</strong> No first solution is perfect. Learn from feedback and improve.</li>
</ul>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Foster collaboration:</strong> Software is a team sport. Designs survive only with engineering and product partnership.</li>
</ul>

<p>
Once the principles were agreed on, I iterated on visual directions for the core screens: the table view, the tracking page, and the contact profile. After several rounds of feedback the team settled on the final style.
</p>

#### From Design to Code
## The system became more valuable to engineering as more of it moved out of the design tool and into the codebase.

<p>
Building the system took several years and went through three main shifts in tooling and architecture.
</p>

<p>
<strong>The Sketch era</strong><br />
The first version of the design system was built in Sketch. Each UI element was its own library, with a master file as the entry point. The system covered four main component categories.
</p>

<Images name={props.images.md["Components-1"]} captions="The initial Sketch design system."/>

<p>
After about two years of running this in production, two problems became hard to ignore. Component updates were expensive: each change meant switching between files, and components could easily drift out of date. Sketch's prototyping capabilities also became too limited, forcing me to use a second tool for anything beyond static screens.
</p>

<p>
Alongside the design tool itself, I built a custom documentation site to bring developers into the system. Despite the effort, adoption stayed low. Design lived in Sketch, code lived in the codebase, and the documentation site sat in between without becoming central to either workflow.
</p>

</section>

<p>
<strong>Figma migration</strong><br />
In early 2023 I migrated the entire system to Figma. The migration was driven by two concrete problems with Sketch: performance had degraded to the point where the file-switching workflow was a daily cost, and Sketch lacked features that had become essential by then, including variables. Figma also meant the whole system could live in a single file without the performance hit.
</p>

<Images name={props.images.md["master-file-2"]} className="mt-5" captions="Contrary to Sketch, all components could be stored in a single file with no visible performance impact."/>

<p>
<strong>Design tokens in the codebase</strong><br />
The migration was a prerequisite for the harder problem: bringing the design system's foundation into the codebase itself.
</p>

<p>
I defined the design tokens (colors, type scale, spacing, border radius, shadows, …) as the source of truth and shipped them into the frontend codebase as code-level primitives. From that point forward, the foundation of the system existed in code, not only in design files.
</p>

<Images 
    containerType="full" 
    className="bg-gray-0" 
    containerWidth="Xl"
    name={props.images.xl["token_architecture_diagram2"]}
    captions="Tokens are defined once in TypeScript, then exported as both a JS object for React and SCSS variables for stylesheets."
/>

<p>
<strong>Linting against hard-coded values</strong><br />
Tokens in code aren't worth much if engineers can still type #3B82F6 into a CSS file. I authored the lint rules that flag any hard-coded color, spacing, typography, … value in the codebase. The rules block the merge unless the value comes from the token set.
</p>

<p>
This also removed a significant amount of design-review work that used to go into catching small visual inconsistencies.
</p>

```javascript
'scale-unlimited/declaration-strict-value': [
    [
        'color',
        'background',
        'background-color',
        'border-color',
        '/^(padding|margin)/',
        'border-radius',
        'font-size',
        'font-weight',
    ],
    {
        ignoreValues: [
            'currentColor',
            'inherit',
            'transparent',
            'auto',
            'none',
            0,
            '/calc/',
            'unset',
        ],
        message:
            'Expected ${types} (expect for padding/margin/border-radius/font-weight) for "${value}" of "${property}"',
    },
],
```
<CodeCaption>The rule that makes the system enforceable. Color, spacing, typography, and border values must come from tokens. If they don't, the build fails.</CodeCaption>


<p>
<strong>Storybook as the documentation layer</strong><br />
The earlier custom documentation site had failed because it sat outside the engineering workflow. I moved component documentation into Storybook and integrated it directly into the frontend application. Components could now be browsed, tested in isolation, and referenced by engineers without leaving their environment. Adoption improved meaningfully.
</p>

<Images name={props.images.md["live-compoenents"]} captions="Using a documentation website directly integrated into the codebase was an essential step in promoting adoption."/>

<p>
A second benefit only became fully visible later. By living inside the codebase as structured, machine-readable component documentation, Storybook also became a foundation that AI tools can consume, and a key piece of infrastructure that the agentic direction builds on directly.
</p>

<section id="future_improvements">

#### Future improvements
## The next architectural step is unifying the design-system foundation across design and code, and using that foundation as the surface AI tools generate against.

<p>
The token layer and the lint rules close the design-code gap at the foundation, since any color, spacing, typography, or related decision in code has to come from the system. However, design and code still live in two largely separate environments above that foundation. The immediate next step is closing that remaining gap.
</p>

<p>
This means turning the design tokens into a true single source of truth shared between Figma and code, by automatically syncing token definitions between the two. It also means applying the same treatment to components, using Figma Code Connect to map Figma components to their production code equivalents, so that designers and engineers reference the same components rather than parallel implementations of them. That unification is what makes the agentic direction credible rather than aspirational.
</p>

<p>
The agentic work has already started. Most teams today use AI to generate isolated mockups or scaffolding, when the more interesting problem is upstream: restructuring the design system itself so AI tools can read its tokens, components, and rules, and produce full designs and prototypes that conform to it. Early prototypes generated against the existing design system have been shared with PMs and engineers internally, and Storybook is the key piece of infrastructure that makes this possible, since it gives AI tools a structured, machine-readable surface to generate against. The longer-term goal is to compress the design-to-prototype cycle dramatically, and to generate production-ready (or close to production-ready) designs that use the actual components and tokens of the system rather than approximations of them.
</p>

<p>
Although still experimental, this will undoubtedly be the next major version of the system.
</p>

</section>

<section id="conclusion">

#### Conclusion

<p>
Creating a design system was necessary to address the scaling and consistency issues that drove the product redesign. What started as a Sketch library has grown into a token-enforced, code-integrated, Storybook-documented system that is now moving toward a fully shared design and code foundation and, on top of that, toward AI-assisted generation. Every architectural step solved a real problem and created the conditions for the next one.
</p>

<ul>
    <li><strong>As a designer</strong>, leading the system has let me produce mockups significantly faster and with higher precision than would otherwise be possible, and has helped me become more engineering-minded when designing.</li>
    <li><strong>For developers</strong>, clear component documentation, reusable patterns, and lint rules that enforce the token system let them focus on logic, tests, and performance instead of endlessly recreating style for every shipped feature.</li>
    <li><strong>For the business</strong>, the productivity gains in new feature delivery have justified the investment many times over, and the system has become an integral part of the product's experience and identity.</li>
</ul>
