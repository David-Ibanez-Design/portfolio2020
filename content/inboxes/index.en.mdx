#### Context
## Our customers needed a way to connect their email inbox to Digima so that leads replies could be registered in the application.

<p>
With approximately 1.7 million emails sent per month and about 6000 regular users, the Digima email feature was by far the most used feature of the product. 
</p>

<p>
However because email replies could not be registered in Digima, users had no choice but to constantly check their email client for lead replies which lead to obvious inefficiencies and in some cases important information loss. 
</p>

<p>
In addition, the business team was planning to transform the product from a simple CRM that was collecting and displaying contact information to one that would act more like an assistant, giving users useful advice about what lead to reach out to, when and how. The ability of registering customer’ email replies in Digima was going to play an important part in achieve that vision.
</p>

<p>
To address the above described issues, it was decided that a feature allowing users to connect their email inbox to Digima needed to be designed and developed. 
</p>

</section>

<section id="research-planning" data-title="Research planning">

#### Research planning
## Spending time planning the research and defining its goals helped the team and I stay focused throughout the research process.

<p>
Given the number of users impacted by this feature and its complexity, understanding the problem space before starting design or development was essential.
</p>

<p>
I started that process by defining the research goals. To that end I organized and lead multiple stakeholders meetings during which the team and I worked on verbalizing our assumptions and questions about the feature, its value to the business and the users as well as its perceived goals.
</p>

<p>
Once we gather enough information, themes started to emerge. After some refinement and prioritization I transformed these themes into 2 main research goals:
</p>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Goal 1</strong>: Understand how users currently use their inboxes</li>
    <li><strong>Goal 2</strong>: Identify technical and user related challenges that would make connecting to inboxes difficult.</li>
</ul>

<section id="research-insights" data-title="Research Insights">

#### Research Insights
## The various researches unearthed a series of insights that later created important design opportunities. 

<p>
Once the research goals were identified, I then listed questions that needed to be answer under each research goals. Research questions were then matched with one or more research methods such as user interviews, surveys, data analysis, technical investigation and so on. This process ensured that research efforts were inline with business and user goals.
</p>

<p>
Here are some of the researches key applicable findings:
</p>


<p><b>From the interviews I found out that: </b> </p>
    <ul>
        <li>6 out of 7 interviewees didn’t know what their email provider were or even what the term “email provider” actually meant</li>
        <li>All interviewees had at least 2 different email addresses. The most common setup was an email address named after its owner and used to interact with leads and an info@../contact@.. email address dedicated to new leads. This email address is usually shared within the company</li>
        <li>I also checked the opposite side: did users know their own email address and password? Everyone knew their address and was able to find their password when needed, even when they couldn't recall it from memory. That finding is what later made an email-and-password-only flow a safe bet.</li>
    </ul>


<p><b>From the survey I found out that: </b></p>
    <ul>
        <li>Almost all participants were highly engaged with their inboxes. 97.6% reported that they checked it multiple times a day, more than 60% reported using some kind of management system (folders, labels etc…).</li>
        <li>60% of users had their inbox set up by a co-worker or an external company</li>
    </ul>

<p> <b>From the data analysis research, I found out that: </b></p>
    <ul>
        <li>35.2% of users used email providers with predictable SMTP/IMAP/POP settings that can be pre-set automatically</li>
        <li>50.6% of users used providers that require custom server names that cannot be predicted</li>
        <li>89% of accounts had a main email address domain that was used by most of it users</li>
        <li>Previous surveys shown that 84% of users were using either Outlook, Gmail or Thunderbird as their email client</li>
    </ul>

<p> <b>From the technical investigation, I found out that:</b></p>
<ul>
    <li>Connecting Gmail users using Google’s dedicated sign-in API required a yearly paid security audit</li>
    <li>Importing user email server settings from their email client was estimated as technically challenging and time consuming to implement and maintain</li>
</ul>

<section id="challenges" data-title="Challenges">

#### Challenges
## Identifying potential challenges was essential so that along with insights gathered from the various researches, they could be used to inform future design solutions.

<p>
Based on the research and stakeholder meetings, I identified the following challenges:
</p>

<p> <b>From the business team: </b></p>
    <ul>
        <li>The product team wanted to release the feature as soon as possible to catch-up with competitors.</li>
    </ul>

<p> <b>From the users</b></p>
    <ul>
        <li>The research shown that most users had little to no knowledge on what email providers they were using or what their email servers settings were.</li>
    </ul>

<p> <b>From the developement team</b></p>
<ul>
    <li>The variety of email providers to support as well as the tight deadline were identified as the main challenges.</li>
</ul>

<p> <b>From the design team (me)</b></p>
<ul>
    <li>Finding ways to help non tech-savvy users connect to their inboxes was the number one challenge.</li>
</ul>

</section>

<section id="problem-statement" data-title="Problem Statement">

#### Problem statement
## Clearly describing the issues I was trying to address early on, helped me stay focused on the most challenging part of the design.

<p>
After gathering research finding and defining challenges, I decided to synthesize those insights into a simple problem statement so that design goals could be clearly identified and communicated to the whole team.
</p>

<p>
“How might we design a feature that would allow non-technically savvy users to connect their inbox to Digima by themselves, while minimizing impact on development time”.
</p>

<section id="ideation" data-title="Ideation">

#### Ideation
## Using the insight gathered from the research, I then started iterating on a few design solutions.

<p>
With the design goals clearly defined and the research insights in mind, I then started to sketch a few potential design solutions.
</p>

<p>
One of my early ideas was to leverage email providers login APIs when available to allow users to connect their inbox to Digima without requiring them to enter any server settings.
</p>

<p>
Before committing to this direction, I ran two parallel investigations to validate it, and both killed the idea. Technical feasibility surfaced a recurring paid security audit required for Google sign-in that would have wiped the feature's ROI. In parallel, user interviews revealed that 6 of 7 participants couldn't even name their email provider, meaning a flow that asked them to "select your provider and sign in" would have failed before it started. Catching this pre-development forced a redesign rooted in what users actually knew, which led to the domain-detection approach that ultimately shipped.
</p>

    withZoom
    className="bg-gray-0"

<p>
Since I couldn’t ask users to select an email provider or use providers’s sign-in APIs I needed to find another way to help users find their email settings. 
</p>

<p>
According to the research, 84% of users were using either Outlook, Gmail or Thunderbird as their email client. Since these tools were storing the same email setting information we needed to connect Digima to user’s inboxes, my next idea was to build a feature that would detect the user client and try to import these setting directly into Digima.
</p>

<p>
The development team however estimated that this feature although feasible, would require a significant amount of time to build and maintain especially giving the fact that clients like Outlook had multiple versions.
</p>

<Images 
    withZoom
    className="bg-gray-0" 
    containerType="full" 
    containerWidth="Xl" 
    name={props.images.xl["iteration-v2"]}
/>

<p>
Taking advantage of the fact that the same email domain was used by a majority of users within a given account, I finally settled on a version that would allow users or the support team to set account-wide email server settings that users could automatically use to connect user's inbox to Digima during the user inbox connection process.
</p>

<p>
The minority of users who knew their email settings could still manually add them.
</p>

<p>
For users who didn’t have access to account level server settings, a dedicated support article was created to help users find them using their email client.
</p>

#### Usability test
## Our testing sessions uncovered a series of usability issues that I were later able to fix.

<p>
Once I was confident that the version I designed was meeting our goals, I set out to validate my assumptions by testing them with real users.
</p>


<p>
To that end, I created a fully functional coded prototype of the user setting flow that we tested with 5 participants recruited from our existing user base.
</p>

<p>
The design had two connection paths, and I tested both. The main path, where a user enters only their email and password. And the fallback path, where a user enters server settings manually.
I only tested the settings flow, and that was a deliberate call. Time and resources were limited, so I had to choose where testing would matter most. The interviews had shown the main path asked only for things users already knew, so I was confident that wasn't where the risk lived. The settings flow was where a non-technical user was most likely to get stuck, so that's where I spent the testing budget.
</p>

<p>
During the test, participants were asked to go through part of the inbox set-up process while verbalizing their thoughts. Some follow-up questions such as “why” , “what do you think of this function” , “tell me more about that”, were asked to try to get users to elaborate on their feelings and rate their experience for each step of the process. 
</p>

<p>
The testing sessions uncovered a series of usability issues that I was able to fix and validate with further testing.
</p>

<Images withZoom name={props.images.md["user-testing-spreadsheet"]} captions="Results of each session were consolidated into a Google spreadsheet for further analysis."/>

<p>
For instance, in the initial design, some participants didn’t noticed the help link that was supposed to open a separate support page with a step by step guide on how to setup their email settings and therefore couldn’t complete the form. 
</p>

<p>
Changing the link into a banner, displaying the help link in error and warning messages as well as moving the help page from the support site directly into the application seems to fix the issue for most participants.
</p>

<section id="conclusion" data-title="Conclusion">

#### Conclusion
### 3 months after the feature release, 65% of users successfully connected their inboxes and emails sending were up by 25%.
<p>
For users, this meant lead replies now appeared directly in Digima, removing the constant inbox-checking loop and the risk of replies slipping through unnoticed. For the business, hitting 65% account adoption within 3 months created the data foundation needed for the product's planned shift toward an assistant-style CRM: reply data that wasn't being captured before, and that opens up advice-based feature directions going forward.
</p>

### What worked: a documented research process kept the team aligned across 4 months of delivery
<p>
Documenting research goals upfront made the work shareable across stakeholders and helped me stay focused throughout.
</p>

### Efforts to make the research process more efficient should be made.
<p>
Even though the insights gathered from the research were instrumental in designing a feature that helped us meet our goals, the overall process took almost 2 months from planning to final recommendations.
</p>

### Taking the time to build a simple proof-of-concept would have helped reduced last minute changes.
<p>
Even though we conducted a lot of technical investigations during the research and design phase, there were still a lot of detail missing and that ended-up causing issues during development. A simple proof of concept would have probably helped us reduce risks even further.
</p>
